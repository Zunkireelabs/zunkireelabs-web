# Google AI Overviews Antitrust Ruling: What It Means for GEO

URL: https://zunkireelabs.com/blog/google-ai-overviews-antitrust-ruling-what-it-means-for-ai-search-and-geo/
Published: 2026-10-01
Summary: A judge dismissed Chegg and Penske's antitrust suits over Google AI Overviews. What the ruling means and how to optimize for AI search (GEO).

**In short:** A US federal judge dismissed antitrust lawsuits from Chegg and Penske Media over Google's AI Overviews, writing that an expectation of search traffic is not an agreement. The ruling does not settle the fairness debate, so businesses should plan for AI search by publishing clear, citable, well-structured content.

## Key Takeaways

- Judge Amit Mehta ruled the plaintiffs' claims do not succeed under US antitrust law.
- The judge also said he was not unsympathetic to publishers and that antitrust law cannot replace legislative action.
- The EU is considering the same questions, and the UK has ordered an AI opt-out for sites in organic search.
- GEO, generative engine optimization, means making content easy for AI search to find, understand and cite.


## What Happened

A US federal judge has dismissed antitrust lawsuits that Chegg and Penske Media (the parent company of Rolling Stone and Variety) brought against Google over its AI-powered search features. The news was reported this week by [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/google/2026/10/antitrust-lawsuits-targeting-google-ai-search-dismissed-by-federal-judge/) and [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1003589/google-ai-overviews-chegg-penske-lawsuits-dismissed).

The two companies had sued in 2025. According to the reports, Chegg claimed Google illegally scraped its educational content, allowing Gemini models to recreate it and reduce Chegg's traffic. Penske argued it was unfair that sites indexed for organic search also have their content used in AI answers, with no way to opt out. Both said AI Overviews pulled visitors away from their sites.

US District Judge Amit Mehta ruled that this conduct is not illegal under antitrust law. In his words, the plaintiffs had pleaded only an "expectation" that Google would send them search traffic: "But an expectation is not an agreement. It is simply how a general search engine works."

## What the Ruling Does Not Say

It is worth being careful here, because this is easy to over-read. According to the same reports, the judge also wrote that the court "does not treat Plaintiffs' alleged harms lightly" and is not "unsympathetic to the situation publishers now find themselves in." His point was that antitrust law cannot stand in for a legislature deciding how to handle the economic impact of new technology.

So the ruling says that these claims do not succeed under US antitrust law. It does not say AI Overviews are good for publishers, and it does not stop other legal or regulatory routes. The reports note that:

- Publishers may have more luck overseas. The European Commission is currently considering the same questions.
- The UK has ordered Google to provide an AI opt-out for sites that want to remain in organic search results.
- The Verge reports that, according to The Information, Google is paying around 100 publishers for contributions to AI Overviews, AI Mode and Gemini as part of a pilot program.

## What Are AI Overviews, and What Is GEO?

**AI Overviews** are AI-written summaries that appear at the top of some Google search results, drawing on content from across the web and usually linking to sources.

**GEO**, short for generative engine optimization, is the practice of making your content easy for AI search systems to find, understand and cite. It sits alongside traditional SEO and shares many of the same foundations.

The practical point of this ruling for businesses is straightforward: AI-generated answers are now a normal part of search, and the legal route to changing that in the US looks difficult for now. Planning around it is more productive than waiting for it to go away.

## What Businesses Can Do

None of this is magic, and no one can guarantee being cited. But a few habits tend to help content get used by AI search and also serve human readers:

1. **Answer questions directly.** Put a clear, concise answer near the top of a page, then add detail underneath.
2. **Be specific and verifiable.** Name sources, give dates, and avoid vague claims. Content that can be checked is easier to trust.
3. **Use structured data where it fits.** Schema markup such as FAQ, Article and Organization helps machines understand what a page is about.
4. **Keep important pages fresh.** Update dates and facts when things change.
5. **Make your brand unambiguous.** Consistent names, an About page and clear author details help systems connect your content to you.
6. **Measure more than clicks.** If AI answers reduce clicks for some queries, track visibility, brand searches and conversions alongside traffic.

## Should You Block AI Crawlers?

This is a business decision, not a technical default. Blocking can reduce how your content is used by AI products, but it may also reduce the chance of being cited or recommended. The UK's reported opt-out requirement suggests this area may get clearer controls over time. For now, decide page by page: marketing and help content you want cited is different from premium content you sell.

## The Bigger Picture

Courts are one place where the rules of AI search will be set, and lawmakers are another. The judge in this case said antitrust law cannot replace a legislature's decision on how to handle the economic impact of new technology, and Ars Technica notes that lawmakers have been slow to adapt to the AI era. Until that changes, the sensible stance for most businesses is to treat AI search as part of how customers find them, invest in clear, credible, well-structured content, and watch how regulation develops in the EU and UK in particular.
