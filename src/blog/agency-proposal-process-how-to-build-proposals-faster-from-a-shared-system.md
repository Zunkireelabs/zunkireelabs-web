**In short:** Many agencies rebuild every proposal from scratch, copying an old document and editing it by hand. That wastes senior time, produces inconsistent pricing and makes it hard to learn what wins. A shared proposal system stores reusable sections, pricing rules and case examples, assembles a draft from the deal record, routes it for review and tracks versions and client views, so proposals go out faster and better.

## Key Takeaways

- Treat a proposal as **assembled from components,** not written fresh: problem, approach, deliverables, timeline, team, pricing, terms and proof.
- Tie **pricing to capacity and past actuals,** not guesswork.
- Add a **review step** for scope, price and risk before anything is sent.
- **Version every proposal** and record what changed after feedback.
- Send **a shareable view** you can track, and a print or PDF version for formal procurement.
- Feed **win and loss reasons** back into the library.

## The Anatomy of a Proposal

| Section | Purpose | Reusable? |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding of the client | Show you listened | Rewrite per client |
| Approach and methodology | How you will work | Largely reusable |
| Deliverables and exclusions | Define scope | Template with edits |
| Timeline and milestones | Set expectations | Template by project type |
| Team | Credibility and capacity | Pull from profiles |
| Investment | Price and payment terms | Calculated from rules |
| Assumptions and dependencies | Protect the project | Template with edits |
| Proof | Case examples and references | Library |
| Terms | Legal and commercial | Controlled by legal |

Only the first section and parts of the others need real thinking each time. The rest can come from a library that is kept current.

## Build a Component Library

- **Approach blocks** for each service type, written clearly and maintained by the service lead.
- **Deliverable templates** with typical exclusions.
- **Case examples** with permission, tagged by industry and service.
- **Team bios** that stay up to date.
- **Terms** approved by whoever owns legal risk.
- **Pricing models** for fixed fee, time and materials and retainers.

Review the library quarterly. Out-of-date blocks are worse than no blocks.

## Price With Evidence

Pricing is where proposals most often go wrong. Improve it by:

1. **Estimating bottom-up** from tasks and roles, then adding a risk allowance.
2. **Checking capacity,** so you do not sell work you cannot staff.
3. **Comparing with similar past projects:** estimated versus actual hours.
4. **Showing options** such as phases or tiers, rather than one number.
5. **Stating assumptions** that the price depends on.

Connecting proposals to utilisation and time data makes this much easier. See our post on [agency utilization and resource planning](/blog/agency-utilization-and-resourcing-see-overbooked-vs-capacity-in-real-time/).

## Add Review Gates

Before sending, check:

- Scope matches discovery notes.
- Exclusions and assumptions are explicit.
- Price meets margin targets.
- Team is available in the proposed dates.
- Terms are the current approved version.

Make the reviewer responsible and the checklist short.

## Share, Track and Follow Up

- A **public share link** lets the client view the latest version and lets you see when it was opened.
- A **print view** serves procurement teams that need a document.
- **Comments and questions** from the client should be logged to the deal.
- **Follow-up reminders** are set from the date sent.

## Learn From Wins and Losses

Record why each proposal won, lost or stalled: price, timing, fit, competitor or no decision. Review monthly. Over time you will see which sections correlate with wins and where your pricing is out of line.

## Where AI Helps

- **Drafting** the client-understanding section from discovery notes, for human editing.
- **Assembling** a first draft from library blocks.
- **Checking consistency** between scope, timeline and price.
- **Summarising** the client's feedback.

Always have a person responsible for what the agency commits to.
