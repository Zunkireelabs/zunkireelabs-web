**In short:** Education Week reported on 2 November 2026 on an audit by Patrick O'Neill of Ivy Tech Community College. He examined 14 meta-analyses, drawn from nearly 40 that concluded AI helps learning, and argues that they fail basic standards: they pool different kinds of studies and different outcomes, often not academic achievement. Those 14 papers appeared in 11 journals and have been cited more than 3,000 times. The audit is a preprint and has not been through peer review, so it is itself a claim to weigh. But it lands on a point other researchers also make: the evidence for AI in schools is thinner than the headlines suggest.

## Key Takeaways

- A meta-analysis combines many studies into one number. It is only as good as the studies it combines and the care taken to combine comparable ones.
- Per Education Week, O'Neill's audit says the 14 meta-analyses did not define what kind of AI use they studied, mixed outcomes such as knowledge, motivation, engagement and perceived learning into one bucket, and mostly failed to control for other influences.
- Examples cited: a study of AI in elementary PE grouped with simulations for medical students, and a few included studies that did not use AI tools at all.
- He also reports impossible statistics and likely invented references in some of the underlying studies. One of the meta-analyses has been retracted, according to the article.
- A separate Stanford review, cited in the same article, found more than 800 studies of which just 20 had a strong cause-and-effect design, and none involved K-12 students.
- We read Education Week's report, not the audit itself, because the preprint page would not load for us. Details here are as reported.

## What the Audit Says

The reporting describes several recurring problems:

1. **No clear definition of AI use.** A chatbot answering homework questions, an adaptive maths tutor and a medical simulation are very different things. Pooling them gives an average that describes none.
2. **Mismatched outcomes.** Test scores, enjoyment and a student's own sense of how much they learned measure different things.
3. **Mismatched populations.** Primary pupils, university students and trainee professionals were combined.
4. **Publication bias not handled.** Studies that find an effect are more likely to be published, so the pooled picture leans positive.
5. **Weak inputs.** If the underlying studies are poor, combining them does not rescue them.

O'Neill's view, as quoted, is that there is a hunger for this evidence that drives people to publish quickly and overstate the solutions, and that the best research on AI and education has probably not been published yet.

## What Is Still Reasonably Solid

This does not show that AI does not help. It shows that some large summary numbers should not be relied on. Narrower evidence is still useful. Our earlier posts cover [controlled tutoring trials](/blog/ai-personalized-learning-what-tutoring-trials-show/), the [OECD's warning that performance is not the same as learning](/blog/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026-ai-performance-not-learning/) and the [Gallup survey on teacher workload](/blog/ai-teacher-workload-what-the-gallup-survey-shows/). The common thread is that design matters: AI built to teach, with teachers in charge, looks different from AI used as an answer machine.

## How to Judge an AI Education Claim

Before a school, training provider or education consultancy buys on a headline figure:

1. **Which tool, doing what?** A named product used in a described way, not "AI".
2. **Which outcome?** Learning measured by a test, not satisfaction or engagement.
3. **Compared with what?** Look for a comparison group.
4. **Who and for how long?** Check the age group, the sample size and whether results last once the tool is removed.
5. **Who ran the study?** Independent, or the vendor.
6. **Does it match your setting?** Results from a university course may not carry over to a school.

## What It Means for Education Providers

If you run an education business, including a study-abroad or training consultancy, the practical lesson is to be careful what you promise. Describe what your AI does, such as answering enquiries quickly or pointing students to the right documents, and measure it, rather than claiming it improves learning. Our guide to [AI for education consultancies](/blog/ai-for-education-consultancy-student-inquiry-automation-guide/) focuses on that kind of operational use, where results are easier to measure.

## The Short Version

The strongest-sounding statistics about AI and learning rest on research syntheses that, according to a new audit, combine things that should not be combined. The audit is a preprint and still needs scrutiny, but it points at a real weakness. Ask for the specific tool, outcome and comparison, and keep your own claims modest and measurable.
